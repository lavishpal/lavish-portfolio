### Application Process

My application process to LFX Mentorships has a very long history to be honest. As far as I know I applied to the program **8 times and got rejected from 8 of them**. Each time I got rejected I learned things about different projects and tried to understand why I was not the selected mentee. These rejections made me learn the open source philosophy and created a mindset that always tries to be “useful” for the team while creating or improving a project and “never give up”.

### Acceptance

But finally i applied for the 9th time and the notification pop up on my screen at 00:02 am while I was working on the pull request.

I got the intuition to open Gmail to check whether I've been accepted into the Linux Foundation Mentorship. It took me a moment to digest that I had received an interview invitation for the LFX Mentorship under the Kubernetes project with my mentors [**Nate Waddington**](https://github.com/nate-double-u), [**Kat Cosgrove**](https://github.com/katcosgrove), [**Rey Lejano**](https://github.com/reylejano) and [**Xander Grzywinski**](https://github.com/salaxander)

![](/images/blog/lfx-mentorship-1.png)

After receiving the interview email, I completed the task, prepared the document as instructed, sent it to the SIG Docs team, and scheduled the interview.

Interview started with warm welcome and introducing each other and honestly it was a great moment to interviewing with people whom i am really look up to in the community. We started the discussion with the what are the problem i faced in running the Kubernetes Reference Docs Generator, approach to make the reference docs generation easy and i received the positive feedback from all the mentors.

Again a notification pop up on my screen after few days. DAM! It took me a while to digest that I've been accepted as an LFX Mentee under the Kubernetes project. My project aimed to improve the Kubernetes API reference docs generator tool with undocumented failure points that only a few people able to run. I worked on refactoring the code, improving error messages, and updating documentation so anyone on the release team can generate the reference docs confidently.

**Github Link:** [**Issue**](https://github.com/kubernetes-sigs/reference-docs/issues/398)**,** [**Project**](https://github.com/kubernetes-sigs/reference-docs)

![](/images/blog/lfx-mentorship-2.png)

Achieving acceptance into the mentorship program doesn't come with shortcuts. Every mentee including me has to go through the project and try to understand the problem statement you'll be working on during your mentorship. Once the problem statement is understood, the next step is to do your homework - try to understand the project, get yourself familiar with the codebase, and so on. The most important thing of all this is to keep connected with your mentors irrespective of whether you get selected or not. The main goal of mentorship should be to gain knowledge rather than target getting accepted.

### Mentorship Kicks off

During the start of the mentorship period, I had several conversations with my mentors about the current problems contributors face while working on the reference generator. After receiving positive feedback, I began working on the issues. All of my mentors were very flexible when it came to the time required to complete a task. One of the best things about [**Nate Waddington**](https://github.com/nate-double-u), [**Kat Cosgrove**](https://github.com/katcosgrove), [**Rey Lejano**](https://github.com/reylejano) and [**Xander Grzywinski**](https://github.com/salaxander) was that each of them was completely comfortable with the flexible timelines.

We were all working on the project asynchronously, and there were many times when mentors were busy with other responsibilities. However, none of this stopped any of us from staying up to date with the project.

While the journey was exhilarating, it was not without moments of self-doubt and occasional imposter syndrome. But then [**Nate**](https://github.com/nate-double-u)**,** [**Kat**](https://github.com/katcosgrove) **and** [**Rey**](https://github.com/reylejano) shared something with me:

![](/images/blog/lfx-mentorship-3.png)

During the mentorship phase, I learned a lot of stuff about how API spec (OpenAPI spec v2, aka Swagger) works, how interfaces work, and most importantly about writing clean code. At every step, the mentors made sure to provide proper feedback on my queries and pull requests, like what areas I needed to improve, and so on. Not only this, the mentorship also provided an opportunity to submit a talk for the **Maintainer Track KubeCon+CloudNativeCon Europe 2026** with the mentor.

### Tips for future mentees:

- Be curious to learn and try to understand the project.
- Do your research(homework) before asking question even if they are too basic.
- While submitting the cover letter make sure to outline the approach you will follow to solve that problem and it doesn’t need to be perfect that’s why mentors are there for to guide you.
- Try to be in contact with mentors before and after the mentorship.

### Conclusion

The Mentorship is the valuable opportunity to learn new things and emphasizing personal growth and professionally. Even if you do not get selected in the first attempt like me , try again for the next attempt with a strong profile. For me, this mentorship holds a special place in my career. It didn’t just teach me about a specific tool or framework it taught me lessons about open source, collaboration, discipline, and long-term growth.
